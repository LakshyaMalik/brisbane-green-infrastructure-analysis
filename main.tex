\subsection{Urban vs Outer Brisbane: Welch's \textit{t}-Test}

To test whether inner-city suburbs experience statistically different temperatures from
outer suburbs, we divided the dataset into two zones: 15 inner-Brisbane suburbs
(St Lucia, Brisbane City, Toowong, New Farm, etc.) and all remaining outer suburbs.
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot} shows the distribution of daily maximum temperature for each
zone over the full study period.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{results/q1/Max Temperature-Inner vs Outer Brisbane (2016-2026).png}
    \caption{Boxplot of daily maximum temperature for inner vs outer Brisbane suburbs
             (2016--2026).}
    \label{fig:boxplot}
\end{figure}

A Welch's two-sample \textit{t}-test (which does not assume equal variances) was applied
to compare the two groups. The test yielded:

\begin{equation}
    t = \langle t\text{-stat} \rangle, \quad p = \langle p\text{-value} \rangle
\end{equation}

\subsection{Temperature vs Latitude: OLS Regression}

We fit an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of mean maximum temperature against
suburb latitude to quantify the north--south temperature gradient across Brisbane.
Each suburb contributes one observation (its long-run mean max temp and its latitude),
avoiding pseudoreplication from daily rows.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{results/q1/Mean Max Temperature vs Latitude.png}
    \caption{OLS regression of mean maximum temperature on latitude across Brisbane
             suburbs (2016--2026). Each point is one suburb; the firebrick line is the
             fitted regression.}
    \label{fig:regression}
\end{figure}

The fitted model is:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\text{Mean Max Temp}} = \langle\text{slope}\rangle \times \text{Latitude}
    + \langle\text{intercept}\rangle
    \quad (R^2 = \langle R^2 \rangle,\ p = \langle p\text{-value} \rangle)
\end{equation}
